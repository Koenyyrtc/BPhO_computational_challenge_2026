And here's the presentation for the British physics computational challenge all coded in python

Task 1
This program simulates random walks — a model where a particle moves N steps in completely random directions. Each colourful line is a separate walk, plotted simultaneously. The sliders let you control the number of steps, the step size, and how many walks to generate. 

Task 2

This simulates Brownian motion — a large particle placed at rest in the centre gets bombarded by 80 smaller particles moving at random. Collisions are handled using conservation of momentum, and you can watch the large particle's trail build up in real time. Sliders let you tune the restitution coefficient and the Knudsen redirect rate

Task 3

This plots two key results from quantum physics — first, Planck's blackbody radiation spectrum showing solar irradiance against wavelength at temperatures of 4000, 5000, and 6000 Kelvin. Second, the Einstein model for molar heat capacity, plotted against temperature for seven different materials including gold, copper, and carbon. Both are selectable from a single input prompt

Task 4 

The centrepiece is the live electron animation — photons hit the cathode and you watch individual electrons fire across the vacuum tube, each with a randomised initial velocity drawn from the kinetic energy the photon delivers above the work function. The voltage slider accelerates or decelerates them in real time, and the intensity slider controls how many electrons are emitted per frame. The graph alongside plots stopping voltage against frequency for your chosen metal.

Task 5

This plots the Bohr model emission spectrum for hydrogen, showing photon energy against wavelength across five series — Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, and Pfund. Each vertical line represents a specific electron transition between energy levels. There's also a hover feature — mouse over any line and it tells you the exact series, transition, wavelength, and photon energy

Task 6

The centrepiece of this simulation is the animated electron beam — when you adjust the accelerating voltage, you actually watch a glowing pulse travel from the cathode, accelerate through the anode, strike the graphite target, and then the diffraction rings fade in on the phosphor screen in real time, mimicking exactly how the physical experiment works. The ring radii update with the voltage using de Broglie's equation and the Bragg condition, across two atomic spacings of graphite


Task 7 

The highlight here is the live time evolution animation — the real part of the wavefunction visibly oscillates at its quantum frequency while the probability density overlays it, and you can switch quantum number mid-animation with the slider, instantly changing the oscillation frequency and number of nodes. Alongside that, it plots energy against quantum number and the static probability densities for n equals 1, 2, and 3.

Task 8 

This visualises the mismatch probabilities for classical versus quantum detection of entangled photons, with sliders for Alice's, Bob's, and Eve's polariser angles. The key feature is the Eve intercepts mode — toggle it and a third curve appears on the sweep graph showing how Eve eavesdropping actually raises the detectable mismatch probability, which is exactly how quantum key distribution exposes interception in the real world

Task 9 

This plots fractional wavelength shift, electron recoil speed, and recoil angle all against scattering angle, across five photon energies from 50 to 1000 keV. The standout feature is the live collision animator — drag the slider and you physically watch the scattered photon and recoil electron arrows rotate in real time, with the photon arrow changing colour as it loses energy and the electron arrow brightening as it gains speed.

Task 10 

This renders full 3D visualisations of hydrogenic orbitals using real spherical harmonics and radial wavefunctions solved directly from the Schrödinger equation. The highlight is the 3D glass plot the orbital is built from stacked transparent slices, each pixel's opacity set proportional to the probability density, so you genuinely see through the orbital's shell structure. Sliders for n, l, m, Z, and mass number update everything live.
